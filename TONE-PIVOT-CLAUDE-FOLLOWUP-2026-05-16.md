## 2. Revised gazetteer samples

Both samples from the proposal had AI tics on close inspection. Stripping them.

### Aethelian Basin — revised gazetteer

**Original draft:**
> A three-day sail across, walled by six port cities that do not war but do not trust. The Basin produces no grain of its own, no metal of its own, no oath of its own; it certifies. The triple-seal excommunication — a captain's name read aloud at each of the six customs-houses — has, in the memory of the older clerks, brought four houses to ruin and one to suicide. The water is shallow and forgiving. The credit on it is not.

**Problems.** Two paired em-dashes. The "do not war but do not trust" antithesis. The closing chiasmus inverting "shallow and forgiving" into "the credit is not."

**Revision** (78 words):
> A three-day sail across. Six port cities stand on its shores. They do not make war on one another, and they do not trust one another. The Basin grows no grain, mines no metal, swears no oath of its own; it certifies. The triple-seal excommunication, by which a captain's name is read aloud at each of the six customs-houses, has in the memory of the older clerks brought four houses to ruin and one master to suicide. The water is shallow and forgiving. The credit is long-memoried.

### Lam-Chen Pass — revised gazetteer

**Original draft:**
> One road. Three days under cliff and a day in scree, with no shelter a determined Council cannot find. Ngaru-Bon eats by it; Kheshkai eats off it. The pass has changed hands six times in living memory and never for long: the first thaw belongs to whoever wintered above, and the wintering above kills men. The widow-tax at the Smith-Spring side is collected by a Khen-Po who does not write his name in the register.

**Problems.** The chiasmus *"Ngaru-Bon eats by it; Kheshkai eats off it."* That is the AI rhetorical reflex Perry called out. The colon-as-aphorism in the middle.

**Revision** (76 words):
> One road. Three days under cliff and a day in scree, with no shelter that a determined Council cannot find. Ngaru-Bon depends on the pass for the trade that feeds the plateau; Kheshkai prospers on the traffic that crosses it. The pass has changed hands six times in living memory, and never for long. The first thaw belongs to whoever wintered above, and the wintering above kills men. The widow-tax at the Smith-Spring side is collected by a Khen-Po whose name is not written in the register.

## 3. Tavakh-Qarat in chronicle voice

Treated as the canonical living city. Uses only canonical names and institutions; introduces no new political alignments, no new offices. The new texture is occupational (the soda-ash glassworks, the lung-rot of the senior masters) and human (Kaolana's succession refusal, the ash-women who keep the glasshouse fires for the families that buy the Junior Clerks' alternative-underwriting).

> Tavakh-Qarat is the north-shore port, and the oldest of them. Its commercial quarter sits inside the pale-gold limestone arcades that the Khazadari built when the rate-cycle was still a matter of seasonal correspondence, before the Inner Circle's biennial review acquired its present authority. The Arcade of Scales is where the cross-rate sessions for felt, spice, frankincense, gold, grain, and cinchona are conducted twice a year. A factor who does not adjust his contract terms within thirty days of the published schedule is called rate-deaf. The word is older than the schedule.
>
> Attached to the Arcade is the senior of the four Triple-Seal panels, which sits in the cool season under the procedural authority that the other three ports observe by rotation. Apprentice factors of any house and any civilization may petition to study under Tabbaq ult Halu, the procedural-instructor whose seventh-stroke Filer's Hand has, in three decades, become the cadence by which Basin commerce hears itself. Her drill takes two years. A factor who has not done the drill can read the contract. He cannot close it. The old men in the Khazadari quarter will tell you the rite was once Wazahaki, brought up from the southern delta in the second generation of the Triple-Seal, and they say this with neither pride nor resentment, as one says that the limestone weeps in the spring rain.
>
> The seat of the house called Havrana-Jenga is at Tavakh-Qarat. Kaolana ka-Mbira has held it for a generation. She is not yet old by the Khazadari count of years. Her house counts succession differently, and by that count she is past her time; she has named no heir. The refusal has frozen the house's internal credit and pushed her younger factors toward the Pulino-Mbira coalition in the periphery, which is extending alternative-underwriting at rates the Inner Circle has neither endorsed nor suppressed. The Junior Clerks' Association, which began two generations back as a marginal corrective to the Circle's biennial schedule, now writes fifteen percent of the port's underwriting. The Inner Circle has been silent on this for eleven cycles. The silence is itself a price.
>
> The soda-ash glassworks lie at the back of the periphery, behind the old harbor quarter where the apprentice-stratum lives. The work is hot. The pay is low. The lung-rot kills the senior masters before they can train a fourth cohort, and the masters who survive the dust take a wife from the ash-women and do not live to see the third child. The Arcade does not buy from them. The merchant houses of the outer quarter do. The Tavakh-face old-money cousins who pour their capital into the Junior Clerks' alternative-underwriting books are the same families whose ash-women keep the glasshouse fires, and the Council that meets at the Arcade and the families that meet at the harbor pretend that the connection is not the connection. It is, and is known to be, and the older clerks of the Khazadari quarter have a phrase for it: working pale-gold.

### What this sample is doing

It uses only canonical names. *Tabbaq ult Halu, Havrana-Jenga, Kaolana ka-Mbira, Pulino-Mbira, Junior Clerks' Association, Inner Circle, Filer's Hand, Tavakh-face old-money, Arcade of Scales, Triple-Seal, Wazahaki, second generation of the Triple-Seal* are all in `factions/01_civ_factions/basin.md` already, in those exact spellings. The reference to the rite being Wazahaki-derived in the second generation of the Triple-Seal is from line 101 of that file.

It introduces three new textures, all safe to extend. The soda-ash glasshouses are referenced in canon (line 107: *"the soda-ash glassworks' apprentice-stratum"*) but not described; the *lung-rot* and *ash-women* are mine. The connection between the Junior Clerks' alternative-underwriting capital and the glasshouse family-fires is implicit in the canon's note that the *"Tavakh-face old-money families' marginal cousins maintain the loose social fabric the Arcade's plaza-rate sessions depend on"* (line 107); I made the connection visible. Kaolana's age framing (not yet old by one count, past her time by another) is new but compatible with the canon's note that her succession freeze has lasted a generation.

It carries adult-realist texture without splatter. The lung-rot of the glassmakers, the marriage to ash-women, the dying before the third child — these are the period-real consequences of pre-industrial silica work, and they are stated in the chronicler's voice (short clauses, no dwelling, no sentiment). The wife-and-third-child line is the strongest single point of adult realism in the passage and it is delivered in fourteen words. If you want this dialed up — a named master, a wedding-night drowning, a more explicit register on what the alternative-underwriting capital is actually buying — say so on this specific sample and I will revise.

It carries verdict-line technique. The first paragraph closes on *"The word is older than the schedule."* The second on *"as one says that the limestone weeps in the spring rain."* The third on *"The silence is itself a price."* The fourth on *"the older clerks of the Khazadari quarter have a phrase for it: working pale-gold."* The first three are narrator-voice verdict-lines on institutional facts; the fourth is reported in-world speech. Four closers in four paragraphs is at the upper limit of the budget; in longer canon passages this density would drop.

It contains no em-dashes used as parenthetical or appositive connectors. It contains no "not A, but B" antithesis. It uses parallel structure twice (*"The work is hot. The pay is low. The lung-rot kills..."*; *"the Council that meets at the Arcade and the families that meet at the harbor"*) and both are in service of the meaning, not the rhetoric.

## 4. Research prompt — period-source extracts

Copy-paste this into another tool (or Gemini Deep Research, ChatGPT o-something, Perplexity) to gather authentic period extracts I can use as anchors. The prompt is specific enough to return usable material; if it returns paraphrase or summary, push back and demand quotation.

```
I am writing a fictional historical chronicle in two registers and need authentic
structural and linguistic models from real period sources. Return EXTRACTS, not
summaries. Quotation with citation, in English translation where the original is
not English. I want to read the cadence.

REGISTER A — Chronicle voice. A reliable but opinionated in-world historian,
sixth through fourteenth century Eurasian, writing about institutions, dynasties,
and incidents. Anchors I want to study:

  - Procopius, Wars and Secret History (Greek, 6th c. Byzantine)
  - Tacitus, Annals and Histories (Latin, 1st-2nd c. Roman)
  - Ibn Khaldun, Muqaddimah — the narrative passages, not the meta-theory
    (Arabic, 14th c. North African)
  - Anna Komnene, Alexiad (Greek, 12th c. Byzantine)
  - Bede, Ecclesiastical History (Latin, 8th c. English)
  - Ammianus Marcellinus, Res Gestae (Latin, 4th c. Roman)
  - Geoffrey of Villehardouin on the Fourth Crusade (Old French, 13th c.)
  - Liudprand of Cremona, the Embassy to Constantinople (Latin, 10th c.)
  - Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian — only if you can speak to its
    cadence in good English translation (Chinese, 1st c. BCE Han)

For each source return:
  1. Three short passages (50-200 words each) demonstrating the chronicler's
     typical paragraph shape: how they introduce a person, how they describe
     an institution, how they recount an incident.
  2. Three of the chronicler's closing verdict-lines or aphoristic judgments.
     Tacitus and Procopius are the masters of this. Quote the original sentence
     and the surrounding two sentences.
  3. The chronicler's handling of violence and sex. How is a massacre, a
     poisoning, a court intrigue, or a rape rendered? Quote a specific passage
     for each. What is named directly, what is veiled, what is footnoted by
     the chronicler's silence?
  4. The chronicler's use of named witnesses, lineages, and dates. How is
     authority claimed for unwitnessed events?

REGISTER B — Gazetteer voice. A working merchant's portolan or a customs
clerk's marginal note. Short, dense, neutral on the surface, opinionated by
inclusion. Anchors:

  - Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (Greek, 1st c.)
  - Hudud al-Alam, "The Regions of the World" (Persian, 10th c.)
  - Marco Polo, Travels — the practical sections on harbors, tolls, monopolies
  - Ibn Battuta on ports specifically (Arabic, 14th c.)
  - English customs records and port books, 14th-16th c.
  - Venetian senatorial reports (Relazioni) from the 15th-16th c.
  - The Carta Pisana and other working portolan charts' marginalia

For each:
  1. Two or three extracted port, chokepoint, or city entries, under 100 words
     each. Quote the working clerk's actual sentences.
  2. The vocabulary specific to the trade-document register: terms for cargo,
     tolls, anchorages, monopolies, weights, credit, and the named coin.
  3. How the clerk is neutral on the surface and opinionated by inclusion.
     What does the clerk record that a modern editor would cut, and why?

FOR BOTH REGISTERS, ALSO RETURN:
  1. A blocklist of vocabulary, syntax, and rhetorical patterns that mark
     prose as modern rather than period. And a parallel list of modernisms
     that are acceptable, since my chronicler is writing in English-equivalent
     and total archaism would be unreadable.
  2. A note on punctuation. Confirm that period chroniclers did not use the
     em-dash the way 20th-century essayists and contemporary AI writing use
     it (as a parenthetical or appositive connector). Show what they used
     instead.
  3. Three real historical incidents involving an Adriatic, Hanseatic, or
     Indian Ocean rim port city, matched to a setting of: a Mediterranean-
     scale semi-enclosed sea, six rival ports with no central enforcement,
     trade in salt / metals / spice / grain. Specifically:
       a. A succession freeze inside a great merchant house that destabilizes
          a port's rate-setting.
       b. An institutional substitute (junior coalition, alternative guild,
          informal underwriting circle) growing into a serious challenger.
       c. A commercial excommunication or boycott that killed a port or a
          merchant house. Real cases. Names. Dates.

Return as much extract as you can. I will read all of it. Do not condense.
```
